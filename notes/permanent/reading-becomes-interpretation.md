Narrative Uncertainty Requires Reader Interpretation
Source

Ganguly, Sanghamitra. "Ford's The Good Soldier and the Unreliable Narrator." International Journal of English Language, Literature and Humanities 6, no. 1 (2018): 215–228.

Permanent Idea

Ganguly argues that The Good Soldier requires readers to reconstruct events from John Dowell's fragmented and contradictory narration. Because Dowell's account is incomplete, inconsistent, and continually revised, narrative meaning cannot be received passively. Instead, readers assemble the novel's reality through interpretation.

Ford therefore transforms unreliable narration from a characteristic of the narrator into a narrative method. Rather than providing certainty, the novel invites readers to participate in producing meaning.

Why it Matters

This insight illustrates a defining feature of literary modernism: the rejection of authoritative narration in favour of subjective perception. Rather than presenting truth as fixed and directly accessible, modernist fiction frequently requires readers to construct meaning from ambiguity, uncertainty, and multiple perspectives. Reading becomes an interpretative act in which meaning is actively produced rather than simply received.

Research Reflection

This idea extends beyond The Good Soldier. It suggests that unreliable narration is less concerned with concealing truth than with changing the reader's role. Rather than receiving a finished narrative, readers become active interpreters who assemble meaning from uncertainty. This provides a useful way of understanding how many modernist texts shift authority away from the narrator and towards the reading process itself.
