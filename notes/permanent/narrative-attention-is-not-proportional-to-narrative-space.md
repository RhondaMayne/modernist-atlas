Narrative Attention Is Not Proportional to Narrative Space

Source

Jesse Schotter, “‘Objects Worthy of Attention’: Modernism and the Travel Guide.”

Permanent Idea

Schotter argues that modernist novels, like travel guides, face a problem of selection. Neither can represent everything, so both must choose which people, places, objects, and details are “worthy of attention.” Selection therefore helps organise perception: particular details are brought into view while the larger world necessarily exceeds what can be represented.

Yet the amount of narrative space given to something does not necessarily determine the significance of the attention it receives. A character or object may appear only briefly while being described with considerable social, material, or historical specificity. Narrative brevity should therefore be distinguished from indistinctness.

Why it Matters

This distinction changes how minor characters and apparently incidental details can be read in modernist writing. Brief appearances need not function merely as fragments of an undifferentiated social world. A modernist text can compress substantial meaning into a small amount of narrative space, making the density and specificity of attention as important as its duration.

This also complicates any simple distinction between central and peripheral figures. Sustained narrative attention is one way of producing significance, but concentrated acts of attention may make apparently minor figures equally revealing of the social and historical world the novel constructs.

Research Reflection

This distinction is particularly important for my work on Mrs Lidgett in Jacob’s Room. Schotter argues that Woolf’s “capsule descriptions” of figures such as Mrs Lidgett emphasise their “indistinctness.” Yet Lidgett is narratively brief without being socially, materially, or institutionally indistinct. Woolf specifies her labour, its repetition, her bodily exhaustion, her workplace at the Prudential Society’s office, and her particular response to St Paul’s.

Examining these details, especially the proper noun “Prudential,” opens the passage onto histories of industrial insurance, working-class insecurity, burial, respectability, and profit. Mrs Lidgett therefore suggests that modernist attention can operate through concentration as well as duration. What appears peripheral in narrative scale may nevertheless be densely embedded in the social and institutional structures of the novel.
