Tammy Clewell, “Consolation Refused: Virginia Woolf, the Great War, and Modernist Mourning”

Citation

Clewell, Tammy. “Consolation Refused: Virginia Woolf, the Great War, and Modernist Mourning.” Modern Fiction Studies, vol. 50, no. 1, Spring 2004, pp. 197–223.

Summary

Clewell argues that Virginia Woolf transformed mourning into an ongoing, anticonsolatory practice in response to the First World War. Rejecting earlier biographical criticism that interpreted Woolf’s grief as pathological, she presents Woolf’s refusal of emotional closure as a deliberate modernist and feminist achievement. Jacob’s Room and To the Lighthouse resist religious, patriotic, social, and aesthetic forms that promise to compensate for loss, detach the living from the dead, or convert death into moral and cultural renewal.

In Jacob’s Room, Woolf makes Jacob’s death a formal principle. The novel begins and ends with images of his absence, while its proleptic structure causes the narrative of his life to unfold retrospectively as an elegy (199–200). Its catalogue of tombs, monuments, requiems, epitaphs, and burial customs exposes the consolatory beliefs through which British culture disguises death’s finality and preserves existing social values. Clewell reads Mrs Lidgett’s visit to St Paul’s within this critique. Lidgett’s admiration of the “little angels” and wish for similar figures on her own tomb express a working-class aspiration towards the elaborate funerary display historically associated with wealth. Clewell connects that aspiration with funeral savings, financial anxiety, and commercial exploitation, arguing that consolatory mourning rituals perpetuate class division by promising deferred compensation for hardship (200–01).

Clewell also distinguishes Lidgett’s attachment to traditional burial imagery from the London merchant’s refusal to contemplate death. Woolf navigates between consolatory evasion and modern death denial, forcing readers to confront mortality and wartime loss without symbolic relief (201–02). More broadly, the novel refuses religious immortality, heroic idealisation, masculine comradeship, and other conventions that might make the war culturally bearable. For Clewell, sustaining grief preserves the capacity to recognise the social conditions that produced the conflict and might allow it to recur (202–03).

The article then develops a feminist and ethical account of Woolf’s mourning. The narrator’s hostility towards Jacob rejects the traditionally passive female mourner and converts grief into feminist grievance against the patriarchal values he inherits (203–06). Woolf’s refusal to make Jacob fully knowable also resists the mourner’s incorporation of the dead into a completed representation. Drawing on Jacques Derrida, Clewell argues that this failed mastery preserves Jacob’s otherness and makes mourning an ethical encounter with irrecoverable loss (207–09).

In To the Lighthouse, Woolf subjects art itself to the same scrutiny. “Time Passes” dismantles the pathetic fallacy and refuses the consoling idea that nature participates in human grief or promises renewal after death (209–14). Lily Briscoe’s painting similarly resists becoming a permanent substitute for Mrs Ramsay. Its final line registers the separation between past and present rather than reconciling them into aesthetic wholeness. Woolf’s art preserves loss as an “invisible presence” and asks readers to sustain grief as a continuing responsibility towards private and public memory (214–19).

Central Argument

Woolf’s postwar modernism creates an anticonsolatory practice of mourning that refuses religious transcendence, patriotic idealisation, psychological closure, and aesthetic redemption. By preserving the wounds and alterity of loss, her fiction converts grief into feminist, political, and ethical vigilance. Jacob’s Room exposes the social values sustained by conventional mourning, while To the Lighthouse develops an artistic form that remembers the dead without claiming to recover or replace them.

Strongest Evidence

Clewell argues that Woolf’s fiction rejects “consolatory paradigms” that neutralise grief and perpetuate inherited gender values. She describes this as a refusal of mourning directed towards healing and closure (198–99).

Jacob’s Room is framed by absence. Jacob is missing from the opening beach scene, and his empty shoes become the material residue of his death at the novel’s conclusion. The narrative’s proleptic structure makes his death active throughout the account of his life (199–200).

Mrs Lidgett, exhausted from scrubbing the Prudential’s steps, repeatedly visits St Paul’s and wishes for angels like those she greets there on her own tomb. Clewell connects this desire with working-class funeral savings and the commercial exploitation of mourning customs (200–01).

Clewell argues that the cathedral’s “ghosts of white marble” belong to the symbolic machinery of social “order” and “discipline.” Consolatory death rituals reproduce class hierarchy by offering imagined compensation after death for deprivation endured in life (201).

The London merchant provides an opposing failure. Whereas Lidgett invests in consolatory burial imagery, he treats the cathedral as gloomy and refuses to make time for death. Woolf steers between traditional consolation and modern repression (201–02).

Jacob’s Room withholds the principal resources of postwar consolation: religious immortality, heroic sacrifice, male comradeship, stoical acceptance, and aesthetic resolution. Clewell argues that these were among the cultural resources that had helped to legitimate the war (202).

The narrator’s anger towards Jacob rejects the prohibition against speaking ill of the dead. Her criticism of his misogyny transforms female grief into feminist grievance and prevents elegy from transmitting patriarchal inheritance intact (203–06).

Woolf’s refusal to make Jacob fully knowable prevents narrative representation from compensating for his loss. Clewell connects this formal incompletion with Derrida’s account of mourning that succeeds ethically by failing to assimilate the dead into the mourner’s identity (207–09).

In “Time Passes,” Woolf dismantles the pathetic fallacy and its promise that natural regeneration can redeem human death. The war breaks the imagined correspondence between natural and human orders and exposes the violence involved in projecting human meaning onto nature (209–14).

Lily’s final line does not heal the breach between past and present. It makes their separation formally visible and preserves Mrs Ramsay’s absence within the completed painting (217–19).

Connections

Primary Texts

Virginia Woolf, Jacob’s Room. Clewell’s principal example of anticonsolatory mourning, formal absence, antiheroic elegy, and the social regulation of death.

Virginia Woolf, To the Lighthouse. Woolf extends her critique from social rituals to the consolatory and redemptive capacities of art itself.

Virginia Woolf, Three Guineas. Clewell connects the patriarchal values embodied by Jacob with Woolf’s later analysis of masculinity, war, and women’s postwar exclusion.

Virginia Woolf, “A Sketch of the Past.” Woolf’s account of laying grief to rest is complicated by her continuing attention to the “invisible presences” left by the dead (218–19).

Concepts

Anticonsolatory mourning. Mourning remains active rather than concluding through detachment, substitution, transcendence, or healing.

Formal absence. Jacob’s death is not confined to the ending but structures the novel from its opening pages.

Mourning as social practice. Death rituals are regulated by cultural norms and participate in the reproduction of class and gender relations.

Funeral finance. Working-class expenditure on burial can express dignity, aspiration, and fear while exposing households to financial pressure and commercial exploitation.

Feminist grievance. Woolf makes bereaved anger politically productive by refusing to idealise a dead man who benefited from patriarchal privilege.

Ethical alterity. The dead cannot be fully recovered through memory or representation. Responsible mourning preserves what remains unknowable about them.

Redemptive aesthetics. Art becomes ethically suspect when it converts death into formal harmony, cultural continuity, or the immortality of the artwork.

Thinkers

Jacques Derrida. Clewell uses Derrida’s account of failed mourning to interpret Woolf’s preservation of Jacob’s radical otherness (207–09).

Sigmund Freud. Clewell contrasts normative “working through” with Freud’s wartime use of grief as a means of exposing discredited cultural ideals (202–03).

David Cannadine. His history of British funeral expenditure supports Clewell’s account of mourning as a source of working-class financial anxiety and commercial exploitation (200–01, 219–20 n. 5).

Jay Winter. Clewell positions Woolf against Winter’s account of traditional commemorative forms as resources through which the bereaved could live with and eventually leave behind wartime loss (202).

Jahan Ramazani. His account of hostility towards the dead supports Clewell’s reading of the narrator’s antiheroic treatment of Jacob as a productive release of bereaved anger (205).

Judith Butler. Clewell draws on Butler to connect the cultural prohibition of grief with destructive forms of melancholia and to conceptualise new social forms for expressing loss (205).

Philippe Ariès. His concept of the twentieth-century “dying of death” informs Clewell’s reading of the London merchant’s avoidance of mortality (201).

Theodor Adorno. Clewell relates Woolf’s rejection of transcendent art to Adorno’s demand for forms adequate to manufactured death (214).

Leo Bersani. His “culture of redemption” provides the modernist aesthetic model against which Clewell defines Lily’s resistance to art as compensation (215–16).

Andrew J. Ball. Ball later argues that Clewell conflates refusing consolatory closure with refusing mourning itself. His correction clarifies that Woolf’s fiction prolongs and repeatedly renews mourning rather than abandoning it.

My Research

Clewell provides a useful model for analysing how Woolf makes literary form carry historical loss and how gendered criticism operates through narrative voice. Her account of mourning as a social practice also supports broader work on Woolf’s marginal figures, class relations, and institutional spaces.

Critique

Strengths

Clewell decisively rejects biographical accounts that pathologise Woolf’s grief and restores formal, historical, feminist, and ethical significance to her mourning practices.

She demonstrates how Jacob’s absence operates as a structuring principle rather than a final plot revelation.

Her account of mourning as a socially regulated practice makes class and gender constitutive of commemoration rather than secondary contexts.

She links Woolf’s antiheroic representation of Jacob with a feminist refusal to transmit patriarchal values through elegiac idealisation.

Her movement from Jacob’s Room to To the Lighthouse shows how Woolf’s critique expands from public rituals and monuments to literary and visual form.

The Derridean account of Jacob’s unknowability gives formal incompletion a persuasive ethical significance.

Limitations

Clewell’s description of Woolf’s practice as a “deliberate refusal to mourn” risks conflating the refusal of consolatory closure with the refusal of mourning itself (199). Her later insistence on “sustaining grief” indicates that her argument actually depends on persistent mourning rather than its rejection (219).

Her opposition between consolation and ethically responsible mourning sometimes becomes too absolute. Consolation need not erase loss, foreclose memory, or produce political complacency.

Clewell’s claim that the symbolic resources offering consolation were also those that caused or legitimated the war is powerful but overly comprehensive. Religious, patriotic, and aesthetic forms do not exercise identical historical functions (202).

The article treats the narrator’s feminist hostility towards Jacob as strategically coherent. Woolf’s shifting narration may produce more uncertainty about the source and authority of particular judgements.

Clewell’s reading of Jacob as an embodiment of patriarchal attitudes sits uneasily beside her later insistence that he remains radically other and irreducible to the narrator’s interpretations.

The Derridean account of alterity is illuminating, but it can abstract mourning from the material and classed conditions that shape who is commemorated, how grief is expressed, and which losses enter public memory.

The claim that sustained grief produces political vigilance requires further demonstration. Grief may intensify ethical attention, but it can also immobilise, privatise, or exhaust the mourner.

Reading Lily’s final line primarily as a mark of division between past and present may understate the painting’s completion and the affirmative force of “I have had my vision.”

The analogies with post-Holocaust memorial forms clarify Clewell’s idea of anticonsolatory remembrance, but they risk compressing historically distinct catastrophes and commemorative conditions.

Questions Raised

What is the precise difference between refusing consolation and refusing mourning?

Can consolation coexist with sustained memory and political criticism?

How does formal incompletion create an ethical relation to the dead rather than merely represent loss?

Does the narrator’s hostility towards Jacob constitute feminist mourning, satire, ambivalence, or some combination of these?

How do classed mourning practices complicate Clewell’s predominantly psychoanalytic and ethical account of loss?

What would “sustaining grief” mean as a collective practice rather than an individual psychological prescription?

Does Lily’s painting refuse closure, produce a provisional completion, or do both simultaneously?

Can grief prevent cultural repetition, or does Clewell assume a political efficacy that mourning cannot guarantee?

How can art retain formal pleasure and coherence without turning loss into redemption?

Does an ethical commitment to memory require wounds to remain open indefinitely?
