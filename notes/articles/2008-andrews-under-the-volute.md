Charles Andrews, "Under the Volute: Jacob's Room, Pacifism, and the Church of England"

Citation

Andrews, Charles. "Under the Volute: Jacob's Room, Pacifism, and the Church of England." Virginia Woolf: Art, Education and Internationalism: Selected Papers from the Seventeenth Annual Conference on Virginia Woolf, edited by Diana Royer and Madelyn Detloff, Clemson University Digital Press, 2008, pp. 64–69.

Summary

Andrews reads Jacob's Room through Woolf's pacifism and her ambivalent relationship with the Church of England. He argues that war repeatedly emerges through Woolf's representations of apparently ordinary forms of social organisation. The national church occupies a central place in this critique because it provides comfort and cultural stability while also preserving hierarchy, disciplining citizens, and encouraging national homogeneity (64). St Paul's Cathedral consequently functions as both an Anglican structure and an emblem of British authority.

Andrews interprets Woolf's image of St Paul's as the volute completing London's snail shell as an image of protection, organic growth, and sluggishness (65–66). The cathedral appears to complete an urban structure made by and fitted to human beings. Its prominence in the skyline also allows it to operate as a sign of the secular city and of national identity. Andrews connects this institutional landscape with the conservative Anglicanism of William Ralph Inge, Dean of St Paul's from 1911 to 1934, although Woolf does not name Inge directly (64–65).

Inside the cathedral, the verger embodies the relationship between religious repose and social discipline. Andrews reads his rod and authority over "the order; the discipline" as signs that the Church sanctions the existing hierarchy (67). Mrs Lidgett nevertheless finds real comfort within this ordered space. Andrews argues that her proximity to Wellington's tomb produces a form of "momentary equality." Her lack of knowledge about Wellington becomes both a sign of exclusion from national history and a form of transcendence that diminishes the authority of military commemoration (67–68). He concludes that Mrs Lidgett anticipates the female outsider of Three Guineas, occupying a liminal position between individuality and national assimilation (68).

Central Argument

Woolf develops a pacifist aesthetic by revealing the military foundations of English social and religious organisation. St Paul's condenses this argument: it offers stability, beauty, and rest while simultaneously sustaining class hierarchy, national discipline, and the cultural authority of military history. Mrs Lidgett's presence introduces a working-class perspective that both participates in and unsettles the cathedral's production of national identity.

Strongest Evidence

Andrews argues that the Church of England contributes to national comfort and stability while maintaining order, disciplining citizens, and ensuring homogeneity (64). This establishes the cathedral's ideological doubleness.

St Paul's becomes "the architectural center of the city, an emblem of Anglican Christianity and British authority" (64). Its architectural prominence allows religious and national authority to become mutually reinforcing.

Woolf's image of London as a shell "secreted by man to fit man himself," completed by St Paul's as its volute, presents the city as a protective structure that appears almost organic (65–66). Andrews interprets the snail as an image of security and sluggishness.

The verger, who has "life ironed out beneath him," presides over "the order; the discipline" and "For ever requiem, repose" (67). Andrews reads him as a social arbiter whose rod authorises the existing institutional order.

Mrs Lidgett rests beneath Wellington's tomb although his victories mean nothing to her and she does not know his name. Her attention attaches instead to the little angels opposite (67–68). Andrews argues that this response reduces the emotional power of military history.

Andrews describes Mrs Lidgett as occupying a liminal position between individual marginality and national assimilation, connecting her retrospectively with the Outsider Society of Three Guineas (68).

Connections

Primary Texts

Virginia Woolf, Jacob's Room. St Paul's, the verger, Mrs Lidgett, Wellington's tomb, the omnibus passengers, and the earlier volute or snail-shell image.

Virginia Woolf, Three Guineas. Andrews treats Mrs Lidgett as an early version of the female outsider who remains marginal to patriarchal national institutions.

Virginia Woolf, Between the Acts. Andrews's model of a pacifist aesthetic invites comparison with Woolf's later representation of national history, ritual, and institutional violence.

Joseph Conrad, Heart of Darkness. Andrews contrasts Woolf's ambivalent Thames with Conrad's river leading towards imperial darkness (65–66).

Concepts

Structural violence. Overt military violence remains in the historical background while ordinary systems of discipline and social attrition occupy the foreground.

Institutional consolation. A social institution can meet a genuine emotional or bodily need while preserving the conditions that produced that need.

Commemorative inequality. Sharing a monumental space does not confer equal access to public memory, burial, or historical recognition.

Naturalised institutions. The shell metaphor makes the built city appear organically secreted, potentially concealing the labour, capital, and power that produced it.

Affective counter-reading. Mrs Lidgett responds to the angels and melodies rather than to Wellington's national fame, redistributing significance within the cathedral.

Thinkers

Karl Marx. Andrews invokes religion as the "opium of the people" but resists reducing Mrs Lidgett's consolation to false consciousness (67–68).

Nancy Anne Knowles. Andrews draws on Knowles's distinction between overt violence in the background and structural violence in the foreground (66).

Jane Marcus. Marcus's account of female marginality as a possible basis for collective politics supports Andrews's connection between Mrs Lidgett and the Outsider Society (68).

William Ralph Inge. His conservative Anglicanism supplies Andrews with historical context for St Paul's as an institution invested in static class hierarchy (64–65).

My Research

Mrs Lidgett and the Prudential. Andrews reproduces Woolf's reference to the "Prudential Society's office" but does not identify or investigate the company. His reading moves from Lidgett's exhaustion to the ideological meanings of St Paul's, leaving the commercial and historical significance of her workplace unexplored.

Industrial assurance and working-class death. Andrews's description of Lidgett as exhausted by labour "for the upper classes" (67) obscures the Prudential's specific position as a commercial institution whose Industrial Branch collected small premiums from working-class households for insurance associated especially with death and burial.

Labour and repose. Andrews's account of the Church as both consolatory and disciplinary can clarify the tension between Lidgett's bodily exhaustion and the repose offered within St Paul's. Her need for rest is authentic, but the cathedral does not alter the material conditions that produced it.

The omnibus passengers. Andrews opposes Jacob's private interiority to the multitude's public exteriority (66). Woolf, however, grants the fleeting passengers unseen personal histories, observing that each carries his past "like the leaves of a book known to him by heart." This supports my wider argument that briefly appearing figures possess significance and interiority beyond their narrative duration.

Monumental inequality. Andrews's "momentary equality" requires qualification. Wellington possesses a name, a permanent tomb, military fame, and a place in national memory. Mrs Lidgett occupies the cathedral temporarily and remains identified only by her married surname. Proximity intensifies this inequality rather than resolving it.

Perceptual authority. Andrews calls Lidgett's ignorance of Wellington "part shortcoming and part transcendence" (67–68). Describing it as a shortcoming allows official military knowledge to determine the value of her perception. Her statement that his victories mean nothing to her may instead reveal the limited affective authority of national commemoration.

Critique

Strengths

Andrews gives a persuasive account of the Church of England as simultaneously consolatory and disciplinary.

He situates St Paul's at the intersection of religion, national identity, class hierarchy, and military memory.

His reading of the verger draws attention to the institutional administration of "order," "discipline," and "repose."

He recognises that Mrs Lidgett's response to the angels weakens Wellington's monopoly on meaning within the cathedral.

His account of structural violence offers a useful way of connecting military history with less spectacular forms of social harm.

Limitations

Andrews quotes the Prudential reference without examining the institution, the Industrial Branch, or the working-class burial economy. This is the principal critical gap relevant to the Lidgett article (67–68).

His description of Lidgett's labour as labour "for the upper classes" replaces a historically specific corporate relationship with a general class binary (67).

"Momentary equality" confuses spatial proximity with equal access to commemoration. The Duke's monumental permanence and Lidgett's temporary rest remain radically asymmetrical (67–68).

Calling Lidgett's ignorance a "shortcoming" accepts military-national knowledge as a normative standard. Calling it "transcendence" risks spiritualising her response and detaching it from her material exhaustion (68).

The claim that Jacob possesses private interiority while the multitude occupies public exteriority underestimates the individual histories Woolf attributes to fleeting passengers (66).

Andrews's conversion of the shell metaphor into the claim that Londoners are slow, soft, timid, and ineffectual presses the image too literally (65–66).

His claim that the cathedral's urban prominence diminishes its religious meaning creates an unnecessary opposition between sacred and secular authority (66).

The connection with Dean Inge is historically suggestive but remains inferential because Woolf does not name him (64–65).

"Petty tyrant" intensifies the representation of the verger beyond what is necessary to establish his institutional authority (67).

The claim that Mrs Lidgett prefigures the Outsider Society is retrospective and potentially teleological. Marginality does not by itself establish conscious political resistance (68).

The description of Lidgett as a "homely figure" risks sentimentalising and domesticating a working-class labourer (68).

Questions Raised

How can a religious institution provide genuine consolation while preserving the hierarchy responsible for a subject's exhaustion?

Does Mrs Lidgett's access to St Paul's constitute inclusion, or does it make the limits of inclusion more visible?

What changes when the Prudential is treated as a historically specific industrial-assurance company rather than a generic upper-class workplace?

How does the contrast between Wellington's permanent tomb and Lidgett's temporary seat organise the scene's account of class and commemoration?

Does "his victories meant nothing to her" signify ignorance, exclusion, indifference, or an alternative form of perceptual authority?

How does the phrase "year in year out" translate structural violence into temporal and bodily terms?

Does the verger produce Lidgett's repose, regulate it, or merely represent the institutional order within which she experiences it?

How does Woolf's attribution of private histories to the omnibus passengers complicate Andrews's opposition between Jacob and the multitude?

Can Mrs Lidgett be connected with the Outsider Society without turning her into a retrospectively constructed political type?

Candidate permanent notes:

Institutional consolation can preserve social hierarchy. Genuine comfort does not necessarily challenge the institution that administers it or the conditions that made it necessary.

Shared monumental space does not produce commemorative equality. Physical proximity to an elite monument may reveal rather than overcome unequal access to public memory.

Structural violence appears through duration. Repetition such as "year in year out" gives slow social attrition a temporal form distinct from spectacular military violence.
