Andrew J. Ball, “Redemptive Mourning: Virginia Woolf’s Transformation of the Elegiac Form”

Citation

Ball, Andrew J. “Redemptive Mourning: Virginia Woolf’s Transformation of the Elegiac Form.” Journal of Comparative Literature and Aesthetics, vol. 43, no. 3, Autumn 2020, pp. 23–33.

Summary

Ball argues that Virginia Woolf does not abandon or wholly subvert the elegiac tradition. She transforms it into a counter-elegiac or novel-elegiac form capable of mourning without conventional consolation. Traditional elegies typically move from grief towards resolution by idealising the dead, imagining their transcendence, or substituting the permanence of art for mortal life. Woolf’s elegies resist this progression. They preserve the dead person’s radical alterity, defer closure, and make the mourner’s continuing responsibility to the past the basis of an ethical relation (23–24).

Ball begins by challenging critical accounts that equate Woolf’s refusal of consolation with an inability or refusal to mourn. He argues that Tammy Clewell identifies the importance of sustained grief but weakens her formulation by describing Woolf’s practice as a “deliberate refusal to mourn.” Woolf instead creates a form of “radically persistent grieving” in which mourning continues without severing attachment to the dead. This interminable memory, rather than consolatory forgetting, provides the possibility of redemption (24).

Drawing on Jacques Derrida, Emmanuel Levinas, Paul Ricoeur, and Clifton Spargo, Ball presents mourning as an encounter with the irreducible otherness of the dead. Conventional elegy seeks to overcome the asymmetry between mourner and mourned through a “fantasy of reciprocity,” idealising the lost person and converting that person into a symbol responsive to the mourner’s needs. Woolf’s counter-elegies refuse this compensatory projection. Their failures of knowledge, intimacy, representation, and closure become ethical forms of restraint (24–29).

Jacob’s Room supplies Ball’s primary example. Jacob’s absence frames the novel from the opening beach scene to the vacant room at its conclusion. The episodic narrative, ambiguous narration, and refusal of interior access prevent readers from converting Jacob into a stable or fully knowable character. Woolf’s assertion that one must “follow hints” becomes both a theory of interpersonal knowledge and a method for reading the novel. This opacity respects Jacob’s autonomy and refuses the idealising intimacy of conventional elegy (25–27).

Ball also reads Jacob’s Room as an antiheroic war elegy. Woolf commemorates an unexceptional life and refuses to transform Jacob’s pointless death into noble military sacrifice. By resisting deification and exposing the “cult of the hero,” the novel challenges a commemorative culture that turns individuals into symbols and uses heroism to justify martial violence. Redemption lies neither in Jacob’s death nor in its aesthetic elevation, but in the mourner’s refusal to forget its injustice (27–29).

The argument then expands across Mrs Dalloway, To the Lighthouse, and The Waves. In Mrs Dalloway, Septimus becomes a prophetic witness whom English society scapegoats to expel its responsibility for wartime violence. Clarissa inherits the task of witnessing his anonymous death, and Woolf’s communal elegy locates redemption in testimony rather than consolation (29–30). To the Lighthouse functions as “writing as survival” for both mourner and mourned. Its repeatable textual form preserves attachment while its anti-pastoral “Time Passes” section rejects the pathetic fallacy and nature’s imagined reciprocity with human grief (30–32). In The Waves, Woolf returns to the cult of the hero through Percival, whose apparent imperial magnificence is exposed as a parodic and politically dangerous construction. Percival’s absence recalls Jacob’s opacity and allows Woolf to continue mourning Thoby without idealising him (32–33).

Central Argument

Woolf renovates the elegy by replacing consolatory closure, heroic idealisation, and the fantasy of reciprocity with persistent mourning, narrative opacity, and vigilant memory. Her counter-elegies respect the radical alterity of the dead and transform mourning into an ethical responsibility to bear witness. Redemption remains possible, but it belongs to the mourner’s continuing openness to an unreconciled past rather than to the recovery, transcendence, or symbolic perfection of the person who died.

Working-through

Strongest Evidence

Ball distinguishes Woolf’s counter-elegies from both traditional elegy and anti-elegy. They reconstruct the genre by retaining mourning while indefinitely deferring its conventional movement towards consolation (23–24).

He corrects the critical conflation of refusing consolation with refusing mourning. Woolf’s open wounds and continuing attachments constitute “radically persistent grieving,” not an absence or failure of grief (24).

Ball uses Derrida’s proposition that mourning “would have to fail in order to succeed” to argue that inconsolability can preserve the dead person’s singularity instead of reducing that person to an object within the mourner’s psychological recovery (24–25).

In Jacob’s Room, the empty room rather than the room itself holds the novel together. Jacob’s absence structures the text, while the narrator’s reliance on “hints” prevents a completed representation from compensating for his loss (25–27).

The novel rejects the “fantasy of reciprocity” through which conventional elegy retrospectively imagines an ideal intimacy with the dead. Jacob remains separate, opaque, and resistant to sentimental idealisation (27).

Woolf commemorates Jacob’s unexceptional life without transforming his wartime death into noble sacrifice. Ball argues that this antiheroic method opposes the “cult of the hero” and the cultural use of elegy to legitimate military violence (27–28).

Ball locates redemption in continued remembrance. The mourner is not redeemed by recovering the dead or closing grief but by remaining open to a past that cannot be made fully present or finally resolved (28–29).

In Mrs Dalloway, Septimus bears witness to the violence his society seeks to disavow. His scapegoating transfers the responsibility for testimony to Clarissa and makes communal mourning an indictment of English cultural amnesia (29–30).

To the Lighthouse preserves the dead through the repeatability of writing while rejecting nature as a consolatory surrogate. “Time Passes” dismantles the pathetic fallacy and presents a natural world indifferent to individual mortality (30–32).

The Waves parodies heroic idealisation through Percival. Ball connects this critique with Woolf’s exposure of the elegy’s role in producing nationalism, war, and fascism (32–33).

Connections

Primary Texts

Virginia Woolf, Jacob’s Room. Ball’s principal example of narrative absence, ethical opacity, antiheroic commemoration, and the rejection of idealised reciprocity.

Virginia Woolf, Mrs Dalloway. The individual elegy becomes communal testimony as Septimus witnesses wartime violence and Clarissa assumes responsibility for remembering his death.

Virginia Woolf, To the Lighthouse. Writing permits survival and continued attachment while “Time Passes” dismantles the pastoral consolations of natural sympathy and renewal.

Virginia Woolf, The Waves. Percival’s heroic and imperial image becomes a parody of elegiac deification and the culture of masculine hero worship.

Virginia Woolf, Three Guineas and The Years. Ball invokes Woolf’s later socialist and antifascist politics to frame her critique of heroism, nationalism, and elegy as an instrument of social control (32).

Virginia Woolf, diaries and letters. Woolf’s statements about laying her parents to rest and repeatedly returning to Thoby allow Ball to distinguish therapeutic working-through from forgetting or completed mourning (30–33).

Concepts

Ethical opacity. A refusal to claim complete knowledge of another person can protect that person from idealisation and narrative mastery.

Redemptive mourning. Redemption arises from remembering without consolation rather than from transcending, replacing, or recovering the dead.

Fantasy of reciprocity. Conventional elegy retrospectively constructs an ideal mutuality with the dead to compensate for the asymmetry and failed intimacy of actual relations.

Antiheroic commemoration. Refusing to idealise the war dead challenges cultural forms that convert meaningless violence into sacrifice, glory, and national renewal.

Writing as survival. The repeatable literary work preserves the mourner’s testimony and the dead person’s trace without claiming to restore the lost life.

Vigilant memory. Continuing openness to historical wounds becomes a form of ethical and political responsibility opposed to silence and cultural amnesia.

Thinkers

Jacques Derrida. Failed mourning succeeds ethically when it refuses to assimilate the dead into the mourner’s recovered identity and preserves the other’s radical alterity (24–25).

Emmanuel Levinas. His account of infinite responsibility and the other’s absolute exteriority supports Ball’s claim that the mourned person remains beyond the mourner’s possession or comprehension (28–30).

Paul Ricoeur. His search for equality within inequality helps Ball consider whether ethical solicitude can respond to asymmetry without declaring reciprocal relation impossible (28–29).

Clifton Spargo. Ball adopts Spargo’s concepts of the “fantasy of reciprocity,” compensatory projection, and the ethical crisis created by loss (24–32).

Tammy Clewell. Ball accepts her account of ongoing grief but argues that she conflates resistance to consolation with a refusal to mourn (24).

Alex Zwerdling. His account of Jacob’s Room as a satiric and antiheroic elegy supports Ball’s critique of reverence, symbolic idealisation, and the glorification of war (25–28).

Celeste M. Schenck. Her feminist reconstruction of elegy helps Ball connect persistent attachment with resistance to the masculine model of redemptive separation (27–28).

Christine Froula. Ball draws on her account of Septimus as scapegoat and prophetic witness but rejects her claim that Clarissa reaches existential consolation (29–30).

Jahan Ramazani. His modern elegy as an “open wound” provides an alternative to consolation, although Ball retains a possibility of redemption that Ramazani’s model resists (30).

Gillian Beer. Ball uses Beer’s “writing as survival” but disputes her suggestion that To the Lighthouse enables Woolf to dispose of family history or complete mourning (30–31).

Jane Goldman and Jane Marcus. Their accounts of Percival and Bernard as parodic hero and poet support Ball’s connection between elegiac idealisation, nationalism, war, and fascism (32).

My Research

Ball offers a conceptual vocabulary for analysing how Woolf makes absence, opacity, repetition, and incomplete knowledge carry ethical significance. His account is useful for broader research on commemoration, war, narrative perspective, and Woolf’s resistance to forms that convert individual lives into representative cultural symbols.

Critique

Strengths

Ball makes a valuable distinction between refusing consolation and refusing mourning. This correction clarifies that persistent attachment can constitute a work of mourning rather than its absence.

He avoids classifying Woolf’s experiments as simply anti-elegiac and instead explains how they retain, criticise, and renovate the genre.

His account of Jacob’s narrative opacity gives formal incompleteness a coherent ethical function.

The “fantasy of reciprocity” provides a strong explanation of why idealising the dead may serve the mourner’s needs while erasing the lost person’s difference.

Ball connects the formal problem of elegy with the political dangers of heroic commemoration, especially the conversion of violent death into sacrifice and national glory.

The movement across four novels demonstrates the continuity and development of Woolf’s elegiac experimentation.

His account of redemption without closure recognises that persistent grief can contain ethical and therapeutic possibilities without requiring forgetting.

Limitations

The category of “redemption” remains unstable. If the mourner is redeemed through refusing consolation, anticonsolatory mourning may become a new consolatory structure under another name.

Ball sometimes treats consolation as equivalent to silence, forgetting, idealisation, and ethical failure. Consolation can coexist with accurate memory, continued attachment, and political criticism.

His account of asymmetry contains a significant contradiction. He argues that alterity must remain irreducible, yet later describes ethical mourning as overcoming asymmetry through a “symmetrically attached” relationship of responsible reciprocity (28–29). The latter formulation risks undoing the separation on which the argument depends.

The claim that the other is radically unknowable can make opacity appear intrinsically ethical. Partial knowledge, embodied familiarity, and relational understanding do not necessarily amount to mastery or appropriation.

Ball’s broad opposition between traditional and modernist elegy simplifies a diverse tradition. Miltonic, Romantic, Victorian, and modern elegies do not all move predictably from despair to idealisation and consolation.

The article often relies on extended philosophical exposition and the interpretations of other critics. Its discussions of Mrs Dalloway and The Waves sometimes summarise existing arguments more than they develop new close readings.

The repeated identification of Jacob, Andrew, and Percival with Thoby risks making Woolf’s fictional characters into biographical substitutes, even as Ball argues against reducing the mourned person to a representative symbol.

The suggestion that Woolf anticipates later phenomenological ethics and thereby proves herself a “vanguard artist and thinker” is admiring but historically imprecise (28).

Ball acknowledges that forgetting may be necessary for survival after trauma but answers the problem by advocating incessant remembrance (33). He does not adequately consider when persistent memory becomes immobilising, compulsive, or psychologically destructive.

The political efficacy of vigilant memory is asserted more than demonstrated. Continued grief may resist cultural amnesia, but it does not automatically produce ethical action or prevent recurrent violence.

Questions Raised

Can mourning be redemptive without redemption functioning as a form of consolation?

What distinguishes persistent ethical remembrance from traumatic fixation or compulsive repetition?

Can the radical asymmetry between self and other coexist with Ball’s model of responsible reciprocity?

Does idealisation always erase alterity, or can commemoration acknowledge admirable qualities without converting a person into a symbol?

Is narrative opacity inherently ethical, or can it also withhold recognition and reproduce indifference?

How much knowledge of another person can elegy claim without turning relation into mastery?

Can a literary text preserve the dead person’s alterity while using that death to produce meaning for living readers?

Does the cult of the hero originate in elegiac form, or does elegy transmit political values generated elsewhere?

What precisely passes from Septimus to Clarissa: grief, knowledge, testimony, responsibility, or symbolic meaning?

Does Woolf’s repeated return to Thoby demonstrate unfinished mourning, artistic transformation, or the continuing availability of family memory as literary material?

How can collective remembrance remain vigilant without imposing permanent woundedness on individual mourners?
